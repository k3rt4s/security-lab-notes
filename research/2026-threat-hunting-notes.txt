# Threat Hunting in the Agentic Age (checked 2026-08-19)

Paraphrased takeaways from two public Black Hills InfoSec / Antisyphon broadcasts:
"Threat Hunting Summit 2026" (full unedited broadcast) and the standalone talk
"Threat Hunting in the Agentic Age" with Faan Rossouw (2026). Own words, not transcript.

## Threat Hunting Summit 2026 - David Bianco keynote, "The Hunter's Paradox"

- David Bianco (Cisco Talos; created the original 2015 "Squirrel" threat hunting loop;
  co-authored the PEAK framework with Splunk in 2023) opened with a reframe: the classic
  definition of hunting - "manual or machine-assisted process for finding what automated
  detection missed" - baked in an assumption that a human must drive the hunt. He argues
  that assumption is now the bottleneck.
- His proposed reframe: think of threat hunting as a reasoning-driven process, not a
  human-driven one. Since LLMs can now do limited reasoning, this opens the door to
  letting AI drive parts of a hunt, not just assist a human running it.
- Why now: volume/velocity of telemetry has outpaced human review capacity (his framing:
  "read all your syslogs every day" was never fully practical and is now impossible at
  scale), and defenders can't just throw more headcount at it.
- The "hunter's paradox": we need AI to keep up with volume, but we can't fully trust it,
  because attackers lie constantly - not just via prompt injection aimed at AI, but via
  deception aimed at anything watching (SIEM, IDS, human analyst). Security telemetry is
  itself typically trustworthy (it reports what happened), but what it reports about can
  be a threat-actor-controlled lie. Humans catch subtle deception better than LLMs, which
  tend to take input at face value.
- Three guardrails he proposes before letting AI drive any hunt:
  1. Tight focus - only hand AI hunts with a well-contained scope, ideally ones you
     already know how to execute but haven't turned into reliable SOC-quality alerts
     (PEAK's "hierarchy of detection outputs" - dashboards/reports are a good AI target,
     a clean detection rule usually isn't a hunt anymore).
  2. Strict guardrails - treat AI hunters as non-human identities: scoped credentials,
     audited access, revocable identity, least privilege - "treat it like a talented but
     very inexperienced new-hire threat hunter."
  3. Graduated autonomy - start the AI as an advisor on narrow hunts with strict access,
     expand scope only as it proves reliable. Don't jump straight to end-state autonomy.
- Hard line: hand over hunts, not the hunting program. Humans must keep deciding what to
  hunt for (strategy/novelty) even as AI executes more of the mechanics (tactics). LLMs
  are bounded by training data and won't propose genuinely novel hunt hypotheses - if you
  let AI set the agenda, you risk converging on well-trodden ground and missing exactly
  the novel attacker behavior hunting exists to catch.
- His own open-source example: the "PEAK Threat Hunting Assistant" (Cisco Talos GitHub)
  targets only the "prepare" phase of PEAK (topic selection, hypothesis generation,
  scoping) with a human-in-the-loop design - can turn planning for a multi-week hunt into
  about an hour of work.
- Model choice: no single "best" model for hunting - depends on data and task; his own
  tooling is model-swappable, and different subtasks in an agentic hunt may warrant
  different models (cheap/local for routine steps, frontier for hard reasoning).
- Cost/architecture point echoed by the panel: don't point an LLM at raw high-volume
  telemetry. Let the SIEM/Splunk do statistical computation (SPL, or offload to a Python
  script); have the LLM interpret results rather than crunch numbers - keeps token cost
  and hallucination risk down.

## Threat Hunting Summit 2026 - Faan Rossouw, "applied harness engineering"

(Overlaps heavily with his standalone talk below; summarized once here, expanded there.)

- Central metaphor (credited to Rob Lee/SANS): a human sees a "deer wearing a mustache"
  and still calls it a deer; deterministic signature matching does not, because it only
  matches the exact pattern trained on. Loosening the signature to catch variants trades
  false negatives for a flood of false positives. Alert-based detection is fundamentally
  "low resolution" (binary alert/no-alert) when reality has a spectrum of "something feels
  off" in between - that spectrum is the traditional domain of human judgment, and is what
  threat hunting exists to work.
- Core thesis: an intentionally designed agentic layer can scale human judgment, not
  replace it. Proposes "applied harness engineering" as the discipline: architect the
  agent/human/deterministic-code system deliberately rather than bolting an LLM onto
  existing workflows.
- Nine components of his system (condensed): (1) how you feed data to agents without
  drowning them (progressive disclosure - context window degrades well before it's full,
  a phenomenon variously called "the dumb zone," "context rot," or "lost in the middle,"
  roughly 40-60% capacity); (2) tools (what an agent can do besides emit text - CLI use,
  APIs, MCP); (3) orchestration patterns (parallel fan-out for independent tasks,
  sequential when output feeds output, "maker-checker"/LLM-as-judge gates before a
  keystone decision cascades); (4) skills (markdown + YAML front matter units that
  concretize a specific hunt procedure - e.g., "hunt C2 over HTTPS on this subnet" - so
  hunts are composable, versionable, and shareable rather than re-typed each time);
  (5) context engineering (delivering org-specific facts - assets, "why is Jane from
  accounting running PowerShell," visibility/coverage gaps, alert history - at inference
  time, since none of that lives in model weights); (6) shared state/knowledge graphs to
  let parallel agent findings connect to each other (cites Google's Sec-Gemini/Timesketch
  work, and Roberto Rodriguez's "Threat Hunters Playbook" as an earlier, non-agentic
  version of the same idea); (7) feedback loops - after every hunt, an agent reviews what
  happened and proposes system improvements (ties directly to PEAK's metrics: missing
  detections, missing telemetry, misconfigurations found even on negative hunts);
  (8) evals to quantify whether a change (model swap, cost optimization) actually helped,
  "beyond vibes"; (9) adversarial resilience - an agentic layer is itself new attack
  surface and needs architectural controls (e.g., dynamic capability scoping), not just
  prompt-level guardrails.
- Practical data-handling pattern: don't feed raw telemetry to an agent and ask "find
  bad stuff." Overlay a "distillation" layer (least-frequency analysis, statistical
  ranking/scoring) that gives the agent a prioritized map to start from - the telemetry
  isn't summarized away, just prioritized.

## Sydney Maroney - "Hunt Amnesia" and the Agentic Threat Hunting Framework (ATHF)

(Sydney Maroney: head of threat hunting at Nebulok; co-founder of Thor Collective;
co-author of PEAK with David Bianco and Ryan Fetterman.)

- Names the real bottleneck on hunting maturity as memory, not tooling or data: teams
  re-run the same hypothesis, walk the same dead end twice, and lose institutional
  knowledge in Slack/Jira/Google Docs where it's technically present but not findable -
  "hunt amnesia."
- PEAK (Prepare, Execute, Act with Knowledge) is the human-run cycle; the knowledge loop
  connecting all three stages is the part teams skip - and it's the part that makes a
  team compound its expertise across quarters instead of restarting from zero each hunt.
- Her Agentic Threat Hunting Framework (ATHF, open source) is not PEAK's replacement -
  it's how AI agents join the PEAK cycle as collaborators. Key insight: AI layered on
  unstructured hunt notes is just a fancy grep; ATHF only works if past hunts are
  structured enough for both humans and models to read reliably.
- The structuring unit is "LOCK" - one markdown file per hunt with four sections:
  Learn (hypothesis/threat context), Observe (baseline normal vs. suspicious), Check
  (queries run and results), Keep (decisions, lessons, follow-on hunts spawned). YAML
  front matter carries machine-readable facts (date, MITRE TTPs, status, results,
  related hunts) so an agent can scan hundreds of hunt files in under a second for
  coverage questions without opening every body.
- Explicitly logs negative findings as valuable: a disproved hypothesis with a
  documented telemetry gap is more useful long-term than an undocumented "found nothing."
- Why markdown over Notion/Confluence/a ticketing system: structured front matter an
  agent can query without hallucinating, predictable section headers, and git-diffable
  history showing exactly what changed and why.
- Field-level advice: status (so an agent doesn't cite half-finished hunts as complete),
  MITRE ATT&CK tactics/techniques (coverage mapping, cross-hunt joins), related-hunts
  links (the graph an agent traverses), true-positive counts (signal-to-noise), and tags
  (soft index) are the fields that matter most - skip them and the agent guesses/
  hallucinates.
- Start small: don't retrofit years of history. Open a repo, write Hunt 0001 in LOCK
  format this week; the framework includes a maturity model that treats AI adoption as
  step two or three, not step one.
- Practical model notes from Q&A: she prefers Claude/Opus for the harder query-and-
  analysis work and would consider cheaper models (Haiku/Sonnet) for lower-stakes tasks
  like report writing to manage cost. No enforced standardization tool beyond the ATHF
  CLI and an agent-readable template reference.

## Active Countermeasures panel - network threat hunting fundamentals

- Reiterated the long-standing rationale for network-layer hunting: malware can hide on
  a host and evade endpoint tooling, but it still has to communicate outward to be
  useful to an attacker - packets on the wire are comparatively hard to fully hide.
  Framed as still-relevant groundwork underneath any agentic hunting layer, not
  something AI replaces.

## Faan Rossouw - "Threat Hunting in the Agentic Age" (standalone Antisyphon talk, 2026)

Fuller version of the harness-engineering material above; notes below are additions or
sharper phrasings not fully covered in the summit recap.

- Three stated principles for "if done correctly": (1) human-agent collaboration is a
  first-class citizen of the architecture from the start, not AI bolted onto an existing
  tool; (2) this is about extending human judgment (augmentation), explicitly not fully
  automated/autonomous hunting - he names Nebulok as a company pursuing the automated-
  hunting path instead; (3) AI is not a universal hammer - most of the actual work
  (data processing, statistics) should still be deterministic code, with agents used
  strategically where reasoning/ambiguity is genuinely required.
- Clean definition worth keeping: "an agent is just a model plus a harness." The model
  (e.g., a frontier LLM) does inference; the harness is the surrounding application code
  that decides what tools it can call, what context it receives, and what it's allowed
  to do. Claude via a browser tab and Claude via Claude Code are the same model, radically
  different agents, because the harness differs. Practically, most teams can only select
  a model (fine-tuning is expensive/impractical for most); the harness is where the real
  engineering leverage is.
- Mechanics of tool use: a model cannot execute anything itself - it can only ask the
  harness "can I run this tool this way?" via a returned JSON-like request; the harness
  decides whether to allow it. This is the loop underlying the "ReAct"/"tau" (think-act-
  observe) pattern.
- Context window mechanics explicit numbers: roughly a 1M-token window as of the talk,
  about ~750,000 words - and performance measurably degrades starting around 40-60% of
  capacity (the "dumb zone" / context rot), independent of whether the window later fills
  and compacts.
- On knowledge: an LLM's knowledge lives entirely in its trained weights, with three
  structural gaps relevant to hunting - stale cutoff date, imperfect accuracy (e.g., it
  usually but not always gets a schema like Sysmon Event ID 3 exactly right, so ground
  truth schemas should be provided at runtime), and zero organizational context by
  default (who's on what host, what's normal for this network) - which is why context
  engineering (RAG, structured docs, DBs) has to supply that at inference time.
- On MCP specifically: framed as a way to wrap an external app's API in a form optimized
  for how agents consume information, distinct from and complementary to raw API calls;
  flagged as an area needing explicit security attention (his workshop covers securing
  MCP specifically).
- Concrete hunt-pipeline architecture he described: Initiation (propose a hypothesis) ->
  fan-out to multiple parallel Detection skills -> each gets an Assessment pass that
  layers in organizational context -> a Narrative/synthesizer agent looks for a common
  thread across assessments (fan-in) -> an Orchestrator agent holds "meta awareness"
  and can act as a maker-checker gate before anything reaches a human -> Feedback agent
  reviews the whole run afterward for system-improvement suggestions (strong PEAK-metric
  parallel: missing detections, missing telemetry, over/under-triggering skills).
- Argues knowledge graphs (e.g., Neo4j-style, "graph RAG") are the efficient way to merge
  many parallel agent outputs when what matters is connections between entities (same
  process spawning the same child process on two different hosts, for example) - much
  more efficient than concatenating multiple markdown reports into one prompt.
- ROI/adoption framing for skeptical leadership: move away from communicating hunting
  value in terms of what excites hunters (intuition, the "aha") and toward metrics
  leadership can weigh - a direct extension of PEAK's reframing of hunting's value as
  "improved security posture found," not just "threats found." He also noted that in his
  experience asking to fund "threat hunting" gets more resistance than asking to fund
  "threat hunting with AI" - a real if slightly cynical lever, and pointed to post-
  Anthropic-incident CISO/Capitol-Hill guidance recommending agentic security workflows
  as one way to borrow authority for the pitch.
- On the "will AI replace analysts" question: framed it as a strategy choice by
  management, not a technology inevitability - if a hunter becomes N-times more
  productive with agentic tooling, an org can either keep the same headcount and cut
  people, or keep people and multiply coverage. His stated experience: no security team
  has ever told him they have "too much" threat hunting coverage, which is why he expects
  (personal opinion, not proven) the coverage-expansion outcome to dominate for hunting
  specifically, even if that doesn't generalize to every AI-touched role.
- Getting-started advice: a functional harness doesn't require custom coding - frameworks
  like LangChain (heavier/more abstracted but fast to a working agent) or deeplearning.ai
  courses (Andrew Ng) are reasonable on-ramps; but he stressed you should still understand
  the underlying loop/architecture even if a framework abstracts it away.
